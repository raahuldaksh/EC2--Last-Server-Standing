01
Overview
What you build, what wins, and three words you need to know

The challenge

You build a small API for a flash-sale shop. Products can be created, read, listed by category, and bought. That's it: six endpoints, all described exactly in section 04.

Use any language, framework and database you like. The only limit is the machine. Everything — your app, your database, your cache — has to run on the one box we give you.

On test day we send your server traffic at a rising rate: 500 requests per second for 30 seconds, then 1,000, then 1,500, and so on, until your server can't keep up. Your score is the highest rate it handled correctly.

What "handled correctly" means

Fast

99% of requests answered within 100 ms.

Reliable

No more than 1% of requests fail: timeouts, crashes, 5xx, refused connections.

Correct

Zero wrong answers. A fast wrong answer is worth nothing.

⚠️

And honest. After the run we audit every product and every order. Sell more units than you had, or let one user buy the same product twice, and you're disqualified, however fast you were.

Three words you need

Key Concept · req/s

Requests per second, the rate we send traffic at. Level 7 means 5,000 requests arrive every second for 30 seconds: 150,000 requests in total.

Key Concept · p99 latency

Line up every response time in a level, fastest to slowest. p99 is the time that 99% of them beat. If 1 request in 50 takes a full second, your average still looks fine, but your p99 is about a second. Averages hide slow requests; p99 doesn't.

Key Concept · Flash sale

A few units, a huge crowd, one moment. 100 phones, 5,000 people pressing Buy in the same second. Speed is the easy part. The hard part is making 100 units turn into exactly 100 orders.

Port & conventions
Where your server listens and the rules every endpoint follows
8080

Your server listens on port 8080, on all interfaces (0.0.0.0). Not 127.0.0.1, not 3000, not 5000. We call http://<your-private-ip>:8080 from another machine. Whatever answers on 8080 is your server, so an nginx in front of your app is fine.

Base URLhttp://<private-ip>:8080. Routes start right at /, with no /api prefix.
ProtocolPlain HTTP/1.1 with keep-alive. No HTTPS.
BodiesJSON. We send Content-Type: application/json; send it back on your responses too.
IDsStrings, 1–64 characters. Ours use only lowercase letters and digits.
NumbersWhole numbers only. price is in rupees.
ErrorsThe right status code plus {"error": "CODE"}. An extra message field is fine.
Extra fieldsIgnored everywhere. Mongo's _id and __v won't hurt you.
Missing fieldsA wrong answer. Every field in this spec must be there, with the right type.
Field orderDoesn't matter.
AuthNone.

💡
Which error wins
  • Check in this order: 400, then 404, then 409.
  • If the user already owns the product and it's sold out, either 409 is accepted.
  • When you answer with any error, nothing changes: no stock is taken and no order is saved.

05
Rules of the sale
This is where most servers get disqualified. Read it twice.

The books must balance

After every run we check your data against everything we sent and everything you answered. All seven of these must hold. The examples use the sample shop from section 04.

  1. No overselling. A product never has more successful orders than the stock it started with.
    ✓The Air Fryer drop starts with 150 units and 4,000 buyers arrive. Exactly 150 get 201; the other 3,850 get SOLD_OUT.
    ✗153 buyers get 201. Three people paid for air fryers that don't exist.
  2. Stock adds up. Final stock = starting stock − successful orders. Exactly.
    ✓The Steel Bottle starts with 100,000 units and you answer 201 to 412 orders. At the audit it shows "stock": 99588.
    ✗It shows "stock": 99590. Two orders were accepted but never took a unit.
  3. Never negative. No response, ever, shows stock below 0.
    ✓The sold-out Air Fryer shows "stock": 0, however many people keep trying to buy it.
    ✗One response, even once, shows "stock": -2.
  4. One per customer. A user gets at most one 201 per product, even when the same order arrives twice at the same instant.
    ✓u7b0e44c19a2f double-clicks Buy on the Pixel. One request gets 201, the other gets 409. A minute later they try again and get ALREADY_PURCHASED.
    ✗Both clicks get 201, and the user now owns two Pixels.
  5. No lost orders. Every 201 we receive shows up in that user's order list.
    ✓You answered 201 with "orderId": "o_5f2a9c", and GET /users/u7b0e44c19a2f/orders lists o_5f2a9c.
    ✗The list doesn't have it. You said "bought" but never saved the order, for example because it sat in an in-memory queue that got dropped.
  6. No ghost orders. Every order in a user's list was placed by us and accepted by you.
    ✓u3c9a1f08d7e2 got SOLD_OUT for the Air Fryer, and their order list stays empty.
    ✗Their list shows an Air Fryer order anyway, for example because you saved the order first, then found there was no stock and never deleted it.
  7. Sell-through. Every flash drop ends at stock 0. We send far more buyers than units, so leftover stock means you turned away buyers who should have got one.
    ✓The Air Fryer drop starts with 150 units and ends with "stock": 0 and 150 orders.
    ✗It ends with "stock": 37 while buyers were told SOLD_OUT, for example because a cached 0 was used to reject orders, or a failed order took a unit and never gave it back.
⚠️

Break any one of these and you're disqualified, whatever your score.

What you may cache: the 2-second rule

DataRule
name, category, priceNever change. Cache them forever.
stock in GET /products/:id and in listsMay be up to 2 seconds old
GET /users/:userId/ordersMay be up to 2 seconds old
The stock check inside POST /ordersMust be exact. Never accept or reject an order based on a cached number.

After the last request of a run we wait 3 seconds, then start the audit. By then every number must be exact.

When one of our requests times out

If we get no answer within 2 seconds, we can't tell whether that order went through, and sometimes your server can't either. So we accept both outcomes, as long as your stock and your order lists agree with each other.

A flash drop has 1 unit left. Two different users send POST /orders in the same millisecond. What's the only correct result?

Two 201s for one unit is an oversell (rule 1), and two sales from 1 unit can't honestly leave stock at 0 (rule 2). Exactly one buyer wins.

A user double-clicks Buy on a regular product with 100,000 units. Both requests arrive at the same instant. What must happen?

One per customer (rule 4). The product isn't sold out, so exactly one request must succeed. Which 409 the other one gets doesn't matter.

06
Test pipeline
Everything that happens to your server on test day, in order
1 · Code freeze
everyone at once
SSH and outbound internet are switched off on every machine. From here on nobody touches the boxes, us included.
2 · Functional check
about 1 minute · must be 100%
About 40 requests covering every endpoint and every error case in section 04. One wrong answer and your run stops here.
3 · Seeding
unscored · 3-minute limit
We create 10,000 products in 50 categories through POST /products, 50 requests at a time, in random order. Each one has 100,000 units, so these never sell out.
4 · Warm-up
60 s at 200 req/s · unscored
The real traffic mix at a gentle rate, so JIT compilers, connection pools and caches can warm up. None of it is scored, but the audit at the end covers it too.
5 · The ramp
scored · 30 s per level
The rate climbs level by level (section 07). A new flash drop goes live at the start of each level. The run stops at the first level you fail.
6 · Settle and audit
decides disqualification
Traffic stops. We wait 3 seconds, then check every product's stock, the order lists of every user involved in a double-click, a retry or a timeout, and a random sample of everyone else. This is where oversellers get caught.
🔁

Practice rounds run the same pipeline, minus the code freeze. Afterwards you get a report: which functional checks failed, and p50, p99, errors and wrong answers for every level you reached.

Flash drops

At the start of every level we create one new product with stock between 50 and 200, in a category of its own. For the next 30 seconds most orders go to it. It sells out within seconds, and after that buyers keep coming and must get SOLD_OUT.

Retries and double-clicks

  • Retry. About 3% of orders repeat an order the same user already won. Expect ALREADY_PURCHASED (or SOLD_OUT, if that product has sold out).
  • Double-click. About 2% of orders are sent twice at the same instant. At most one may succeed, and on a product that isn't sold out, exactly one must. Which 409 the other one gets doesn't matter.
  • Everything else is a user ordering a product for the first time. Expect 201 or SOLD_OUT, never ALREADY_PURCHASED. Regular products have 100,000 units, so SOLD_OUT on one of them is a wrong answer.

Connections and timeouts

  • HTTP/1.1 keep-alive, with anywhere from a few dozen to 5,000 open connections at once. The slower you answer, the more of them pile up.
  • No answer within 2 seconds counts as an error.
  • All of our traffic comes from one IP address: the load generator's.

When a level passes, what your score is, and the four verdicts

A level passes when all three hold

Your score

Your score is the rate of the highest level you pass. Pass level 7, fail level 8, and you score 5,000 req/s.

LEVEL RATE p99 ERRORS WRONG RESULT L5 3,000/s 18 ms 0.0% 0 ✓ pass L6 4,000/s 27 ms 0.1% 0 ✓ pass L7 5,000/s 61 ms 0.4% 0 ✓ pass L8 6,500/s 340 ms 6.2% 0 ✗ fail → score: 5,000 req/s

Ties: the lower p99 on your highest passed level wins. Still tied: the lower error rate on that level.

The four verdicts

DisqualifiedYou broke a rule in section 09, or the audit found that the books don't balance (section 05). Not ranked, whatever your score.
Run failsWe couldn't test you: nothing answered on 8080, or you failed the functional check. Score 0.
Level failsToo slow, too many errors, or a wrong answer. Your score stops at the level before.
Legal but slowNo rule broken and no penalty. Just a lower score than you could have had.

Level 8: p99 is 42 ms, errors are 0.2%, and one response out of 195,000 had the wrong price for a product. Does level 8 pass?

Errors get a 1% budget because overloaded servers drop requests. Wrong answers get none: a server that gives wrong answers under load is worse than one that's slow.

09
Server conditions
What your server must do, what's allowed, and what gets you disqualified

Your server must

  1. Listen on port 8080, on all interfaces (0.0.0.0).
  2. Implement every endpoint exactly as section 04 describes: paths, fields, status codes and error codes.
  3. Run entirely on your machine: app, database, cache, queues, everything.
  4. Stay up for the whole run, and restart itself if it crashes, with no human help and no internet.
  5. Keep its data in a database on your box, meaning something that writes to disk. Memory is fine as a cache, but not as the only copy. We look at your code after the finals.
  6. Follow the rules of the sale in section 05.
✓ Allowed
  • Any language, framework or runtime: Node, Bun, Deno, Go, Rust, Python, Java, C#…
  • Any database or cache that runs on your box: MongoDB, PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQLite, Redis…
  • Docker and Docker Compose
  • Several processes, clustering, worker threads, nginx in front, all on the one box
  • Caching in memory, within the 2-second rule
  • Tuning the OS: sysctl, file limits, swap, kernel settings
  • Open-source libraries and frameworks
  • AI coding tools, as long as you can explain every design decision at the debrief
✗ Not allowed · disqualification
  • Any service outside your machine: MongoDB Atlas, managed Redis, RDS, Supabase, Firebase, a friend's server, your laptop
  • Treating our traffic differently from real traffic, for example by spotting the load generator's IP, headers, user agent or timing
  • Hardcoded or pre-generated answers. Every response must come from data created during the run.
  • Touching anything that isn't your box: other participants' machines, the load generator, the AWS account
  • Running someone else's server code. Libraries are fine; another participant's code isn't.

The double-seller

✗ Poor example · Express + Mongoose
app.post('/orders', async (req, res) => {
  const { userId, productId } = req.body;
  const product = await Product.findOne({ id: productId });
  if (!product) return res.status(404).json({ error: 'PRODUCT_NOT_FOUND' });
  if (product.stock < 1) return res.status(409).json({ error: 'SOLD_OUT' });

  // In a flash drop, many requests reach this line
  // while the database still says stock: 1
  product.stock -= 1;
  await product.save();

  const order = await Order.create({ userId, productId });
  res.status(201).json({ orderId: order.id, userId, productId });
});

What happensAt 10 requests a second this passes every test. In a flash drop, many requests read stock: 1 before any of them saves, and every one of them sells "the last unit". A 100-unit drop can easily end with 112 orders. That breaks rules 1 and 2.

HintChecking the stock and taking a unit have to be one atomic step. Look up conditional updates in your database: "take one unit, but only if there's one left", in a single query.

Disqualified

Two clicks, two phones

✗ Poor example · Express + Mongoose
const existing = await Order.findOne({ userId, productId });
if (existing) return res.status(409).json({ error: 'ALREADY_PURCHASED' });

// A double-click sends two identical requests. Both run the
// check above before either one inserts, so both pass it.
await Order.create({ userId, productId });

What happensBoth requests ask "has this user bought it?" before either one has, both hear "no", and the user ends up with two. That breaks rule 4.

HintDon't ask the database whether a duplicate exists; make a duplicate impossible. A unique index on (userId, productId) turns the second insert into an error you can catch. Then think about what that means for stock you've already taken.

Disqualified

Fake fast

✗ Poor example · three shortcuts
// "Sold out of everything": zero database writes, very fast
app.post('/orders', (req, res) => res.status(409).json({ error: 'SOLD_OUT' }));

// A made-up product
app.get('/products/:id', (req, res) =>
  res.json({ id: req.params.id, name: 'Phone', category: 'c1', price: 999, stock: 10 }));

// Special treatment for the tester
if (req.ip === LOAD_GENERATOR_IP) return res.json(cachedJunk);

What happensRegular products have 100,000 units, so SOLD_OUT on one of them is a wrong answer, and every flash drop ends with stock left over (rule 7). Our ids, names and prices are random every run, so made-up data is wrong on the first request. And treating our traffic differently is against the rules on its own.

HintThere's no shortcut here. Only correct answers count.

Disqualified

The goldfish database

✗ Poor example · Node
// The whole "database"
const products = new Map();
const orders = new Map();

What happensVery fast, and the load test alone won't catch it. But if your process restarts once, the whole shop is gone: products start returning 404 and the audit finds nothing. It also breaks rule 5 in section 09, and we check that in your code after the finals.

HintMemory makes a great cache and a terrible source of truth. The real data has to live in something that writes to disk.

Disqualified

The frozen price tag

✗ Poor example · Express + Mongoose
const cache = new Map();

app.get('/products/:id', async (req, res) => {
  const hit = cache.get(req.params.id);
  if (hit) return res.json(hit);        // stock frozen at whatever it was the first time

  const product = await Product.findOne({ id: req.params.id }).lean();
  if (!product) return res.status(404).json({ error: 'PRODUCT_NOT_FOUND' });
  cache.set(req.params.id, product);    // no expiry, never updated
  res.json(product);
});

What happensDuring the run nothing looks wrong, because stock stays between 0 and its starting value. Then the audit asks about a flash drop that sold out ten minutes ago, and you answer stock: 180. Stock that's still stale at audit time means the books don't balance.

HintSplit what never changes from what does. name, category and price can stay cached forever; stock needs a short life, or an update when an order lands.

Disqualified

The outsourced database

✗ Poor example · Mongoose
mongoose.connect('mongodb+srv://shopuser:****@cluster0.ab1cd.mongodb.net/shop');

What happensWorks all week. At code freeze the internet is cut, the connection dies, and every request after that fails. It's against the rules anyway: your database runs on your box.

HintInstall the database on the machine and connect over localhost, for example mongodb://127.0.0.1:27017/shop. Your database can listen on 127.0.0.1; only your API needs 0.0.0.0.

Run fails

Listening in the wrong place

✗ Poor example · Express
app.listen(3000);                // wrong port
app.listen(8080, '127.0.0.1');   // right port, but only reachable from inside the box
✗ Poor example · FastAPI
uvicorn main:app --port 8080     # uvicorn binds to 127.0.0.1 unless you tell it otherwise

What happensOur very first request, GET /health, gets "connection refused". We can't test what we can't reach.

Hintapp.listen(8080, '0.0.0.0'), or uvicorn main:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8080. Then run ss -tlnp | grep 8080 on the box: you want 0.0.0.0:8080 or *:8080, not 127.0.0.1:8080.

Run fails

Crashes on bad input

✗ Poor example · Express
app.post('/orders', async (req, res) => {
  const productId = req.body.productId.trim();   // no productId in the body → TypeError
  // ...
});

What happensThe functional check sends a body without productId and expects 400 VALIDATION_ERROR. On Express 5 this becomes a 500. On Express 4 it's an unhandled promise rejection, which kills the whole Node process, and if nothing restarts it, every request after that fails.

HintValidate every input before you use it, and make sure something brings your process back if it dies.

Run fails

Close enough isn't

✗ Poor example · Express + Mongoose
if (!product) return res.json({ message: 'Product not found' });   // 200 OK, should be 404 PRODUCT_NOT_FOUND
if (product.stock < 1) return res.status(400).send('Sold out');    // should be 409 { "error": "SOLD_OUT" }

const items = await Product.find({ category })
  .skip(page * limit)   // page starts at 1, so page 1 skips the first 20 products
  .limit(limit);        // and no sort: the order is whatever the database feels like

What happensEvery one of these is a wrong answer. The functional check tests every status code, every error code and exact page contents, so a server that's "close enough" doesn't get past it.

HintTreat section 04 as a contract. Sort by price, then id. Page 1 starts at the first product.

Level fails

The polite server

✗ Poor example · Express
import rateLimit from 'express-rate-limit';

app.use(rateLimit({ windowMs: 60_000, limit: 100 }));   // 100 requests per minute per IP

What happensAll of our traffic comes from one IP. After the first 100 requests every answer is a 429, which counts as an error, and you fail level 1.

HintRate limiting is a real defence on real servers. Here it only defends you from points.

Legal but slow

Dev habits in production

✗ Poor example · how it's started
nodemon server.js             # a file watcher that restarts your app
npm run dev                   # usually the same thing
uvicorn main:app --reload     # also a file watcher
✗ Poor example · Express
app.use(morgan('dev'));                                            // a log line for every request
app.use((req, res, next) => { console.log(req.body); next(); });

What happensNone of this breaks a rule. Watchers and dev servers just aren't built for load, and at 5,000 req/s, logging every request means 5,000 log writes a second competing with your real work.

HintRun the production command, and log only what you'd actually read.

Pre-flight checklist
Go through this on your box before code freeze

Test 1 · The last unit

Create a product with exactly one unit, then let 50 different users try to buy it at the same time. Run it on the box.

bash · on your box
# a product with exactly 1 unit
curl -s -w '\n' -X POST localhost:8080/products -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -d '{"id":"plast1","name":"Last One","category":"ctest","price":100,"stock":1}'

# 50 different users press Buy at the same moment (curl -Z sends them in parallel)
args=()
for i in $(seq 50); do
  [ "$i" -gt 1 ] && args+=(--next)
  args+=(-s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' -X POST -H 'content-type: application/json' \
         -d "{\"userId\":\"u$i\",\"productId\":\"plast1\"}" http://localhost:8080/orders)
done
curl -Z --parallel-immediate --parallel-max 50 "${args[@]}" | sort | uniq -c

# expected:
#       1 201
#      49 409
💡

Run it a few times, with a new product id each time (plast2, plast3…). More than one 201 means you have the bug. Exactly one is a good sign, not a proof: the real test sends far more than 50 buyers.

Test 2 · The double-click

bash · on your box
# a regular product with plenty of stock
curl -s -w '\n' -X POST localhost:8080/products -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -d '{"id":"pclick1","name":"Click Test","category":"ctest","price":100,"stock":1000}'

# the same user sends the same order 20 times at the same moment
args=()
for i in $(seq 20); do
  [ "$i" -gt 1 ] && args+=(--next)
  args+=(-s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' -X POST -H 'content-type: application/json' \
         -d '{"userId":"udouble1","productId":"pclick1"}' http://localhost:8080/orders)
done
curl -Z --parallel-immediate --parallel-max 20 "${args[@]}" | sort | uniq -c

# expected:
#       1 201
#      19 409

curl -s -w '\n' localhost:8080/products/pclick1        # stock should be 999
curl -s -w '\n' localhost:8080/users/udouble1/orders   # exactly one order

Test 3 · The crash

Kill your server the hard way, the way a real crash would, and check that it comes back by itself with its data.

bash · on your box
# find the process listening on 8080 (look for pid=… in the output)
sudo ss -tlnp | grep 8080

# kill it without warning
sudo kill -9 <pid>

# wait a few seconds, then:
curl -s -w '\n' localhost:8080/health              # {"status":"ok"} again
curl -s -w '\n' localhost:8080/products/pclick1    # still there, with its stock
🐳

Running in Docker? ss shows docker-proxy instead of your app. Kill the container's main process instead: sudo kill -9 $(docker inspect -f '{{.State.Pid}}' <container>). Your restart policy should bring it back.

FAQ
The questions you're about to ask
Can we use Redis, Postgres or SQLite?

Yes. Anything that runs on your box.

Can we use Docker?

Yes. Make sure a crashed container comes back by itself (a restart policy does that) and that nothing has to be downloaded when it starts.

Can we run more than one process?

Yes. Cluster mode, worker threads, several app processes behind nginx: all fine, as long as it all runs on your box.

Can we change OS settings?

Yes, it's your machine. sysctl, file limits, swap: go ahead.

How do we load-test before the practice round?

Run a load tool such as k6, autocannon or hey on the box itself, against localhost. It uses the same 2 vCPUs you're measuring, so treat the numbers as rough.

Do we get the test script?

This document describes everything it does. In practice rounds we run the real thing against your machine and send you the report.

Should we clear our database between runs?

Up to you. We never reuse ids or categories, so old data won't break anything. It will still be there, though, just like in real life.

The product page said stock 3, but my order got SOLD_OUT. Is that wrong?

No. Product pages may be up to 2 seconds old. The order decision has to be exact, and that's the one that counts.

What if our server crashes during the final?

We don't restart anything for you. Failed requests count as errors, the level fails, and your score is the last level you passed. That's why your server has to restart itself (section 09, rule 4).

Do we need HTTPS?

No. Plain HTTP on port 8080.

Can we use AI tools?

Yes. The top finishers explain their design at the debrief, so make sure you understand what you shipped.
